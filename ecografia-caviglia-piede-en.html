What it is for

Assesses the ligaments around the ankle bones, the Achilles tendon, the plantar fascia (the band of tissue under the sole of the foot) and the bursae of the foot. Requested after an ankle sprain, for a suspected ligament injury, Achilles tendinopathy or pain in the sole of the foot.

Why it is done

Useful after an ankle sprain, heel or sole pain, or suspected ligament injury.

How it works

You lie on the couch with your foot uncovered. I put gel on the ankle or the sole of the foot, where it hurts, and move the probe. Sometimes I ask you to move your foot or point to the exact spot that hurts, to see the tendons and ligaments in motion.

What I check

I assess the ankle ligaments, the Achilles tendon, the plantar fascia and the bursae of the foot.

Is any preparation needed?

No. This scan needs no fasting and no full bladder. Just make sure the area can be uncovered easily and, if you have them, bring previous reports or scans: they help to compare and follow any change over time.

Frequently asked questions

Is it useful for heel pain?

Often, yes. The most common cause is plantar fasciitis (inflammation of the band of tissue supporting the sole): I measure the thickness of the fascia where it attaches to the heel bone and compare it with the other side. I also look at the Achilles tendon and nearby bursae. If the pain comes from the bone, your doctor may suggest an X-ray.

Is it useful after an ankle sprain?

Very. I study the outer ligaments, in particular the anterior talofibular ligament, on the outer side of the ankle, the one most often injured, any effusion (fluid in the joint) and the state of the peroneal tendons, which run behind the outer ankle bone. If needed, I also assess the Achilles tendon.

Can a Morton's neuroma be seen?

Yes. It is looked for between the heads of the metatarsals, where it causes that burning pain radiating to the toes. Ultrasound detects it and measures its size, and it is well suited to the task because pressure can be applied at the exact spot where it hurts.

Can ultrasound see fractures?

Ultrasound shows tendons, muscles, ligaments and the surface of the bone well, but not the inside of the bone. If a fracture is suspected, the first test is usually an X-ray.

Why is the side that does not hurt sometimes checked too?

Because comparing with the healthy side helps to tell whether a difference is significant. If needed I do it during the same appointment.

Does it hurt?

No, ultrasound does not hurt. It is a painless scan: the probe glides over the skin with a little gel. In some cases the pressure of the probe may cause slight discomfort, for example on an area that is already inflamed, on a very full bladder or on a painful joint. In rare situations, such as appendicitis, the scan may be more uncomfortable, but these cases are rare and the pressure is adapted to what you can tolerate. If you feel pain, tell me: we stop straight away.

How long does an ankle and foot ultrasound take?

About 15–20 minutes; up to 30 if both the ankle and the foot need to be examined, for example ligaments and heel.

What should I wear? Should I avoid creams?

Avoid creams on the ankle and foot on the day of the scan. Remove socks and ankle supports; wear trousers that roll up easily above the ankle.
